\begin{abstract}
% TODO(draft): polish abstract after Introduction is finalized.
This paper presents a passive viscoelastic haptic rendering mechanism in which
an elastic element and a controllable dissipative element are both integrated
into the load-side transmission path and share a common adjustable pivot as the
single modulation source. A single lever ratio $n$ simultaneously co-regulates
the effective stiffness $k_{\mathrm{eq}}=k_s/n^{2}$ and the effective
damping $c_{\mathrm{eq}}=c_{tr}/n^{2}$ rendered to the user;
both quantities scale with the same power of the lever ratio, so the
relaxation time constant $\tau=c_{tr}/k_s$ remains invariant under
modulation. This reproduces the co-varying
stiffness--damping behavior of physical viscoelastic materials such as
biological tissue and NiTi shape-memory alloys, without active velocity
estimation or software-synthesized virtual dampers. The mechanism is
inherently passive, requires no energy injection, and avoids the passivity
constraints and energy artifacts of active impedance control.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
haptic rendering, variable impedance mechanism, viscoelasticity, variable

\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
% ===== Introduction 完整草稿 =====
Physical contact between a human and a tool is never purely elastic. Tissues,
biological organs, and soft materials respond to indentation with both
displacement- and velocity-dependent forces: they store energy like a spring
and dissipate it like a dashpot \cite{fun1980biomechanics}. This combined
viscoelastic signature is essential to the perceptual realism of surgical
simulators, teleoperated palpation, and robotic manipulation of compliant
objects. Reproducing it in hardware, however, is surprisingly difficult.

% 背景：现有变杠杆机构只能做刚度
Variable lever-ratio mechanisms are a compact and effective way to render
displacement-dependent impedance. By translating the pivot of a lever, the
mechanical advantage between the load and the actuator changes continuously,
which modulates the equivalent stiffness seen at the user port over a wide
range without large backdrivable actuation \cite{vanderborght2013variable,
mehling2007increasing}. Yet such mechanisms rely on a single passive
energy-storage element---typically a spring---and therefore render stiffness
only. Velocity-dependent force response, i.e., physical damping, is absent
from the transmission path.

% 现状：阻尼通常靠软件虚拟或主动合成
In most haptic systems, damping is not a physical property of the mechanism
but a quantity synthesized by software. The control loop must estimate the
velocity of the end-effector and command the motor to produce a force
proportional to it. This approach has well-documented drawbacks: velocity
estimation amplifies sensor noise, the synthesized damper can inject energy
into the coupled human--robot system, and strict passivity constraints are
needed to keep the interaction stable \cite{colgate1994factors}. As a result,
the rendered impedance is bounded not only by the actuator but also by the
stability margin of the active loop.

% 第三个问题：刚度与阻尼被当作独立变量
A further gap concerns how stiffness and damping are treated as design
variables. In physical viscoelastic materials, these two quantities are not
independent: they co-vary through shared microstructural properties, e.g.,
the relaxation time constant of biological tissue is largely invariant over a
physiological range of strain rates \cite{fun1980biomechanics}. Across
materials, stiffness and damping cluster along a well-known trade-off
frontier, and within any given viscoelastic system the two quantities are
locked by a common relaxation spectrum rather than set independently
\cite{unwin2018escaping,tsimouri2024lightweight}. Existing
variable impedance mechanisms, however, regulate stiffness and damping
through separate modules or separate control inputs \cite{vanderborght2013variable}.
Even when both quantities can be adjusted, they are treated as independent
design variables, and no single physical parameter links them in the way
that material structure does.

% 本文贡献
To address these gaps, this paper proposes a passive viscoelastic rendering
mechanism based on an integrated dissipative transmission. The contributions
are:

% ①
\textbf{1) Viscoelastic rendering via integrated dissipative transmission.}
A viscous dissipative element is integrated into the load-side transmission
path and its engagement with the load is controllable. The mechanism can
therefore physically render both stiffness and damping from a single
transmission structure, sidestepping active velocity estimation and
software-synthesized virtual dampers with their passivity constraints and
energy artifacts.

% ②
\textbf{2) Functionally decoupled elastic and dissipative elements.}
Elastic energy storage and viscous dissipation are assigned to two distinct
passive mechanical components. Each preserves its intrinsic physical
behavior---the spring stores and returns energy, the damper dissipates
it---without the cross-coupling that arises when both quantities are
synthesized through a single control loop.

% ③
\textbf{3) Single-parameter impedance co-regulation.}
Effective stiffness and damping share the same geometric pivot as the single
modulation source and are continuously and jointly adjusted by the lever
ratio $n$. Since $k_{\mathrm{eq}}=k_s/n^{2}$ and
$c_{\mathrm{eq}}=c_{tr}/n^{2}$, the ratio of damping to
stiffness---and hence the relaxation time constant---is independent of the
pivot position, mirroring the
co-variation of stiffness and damping found in physical viscoelastic media
such as biological tissue, NiTi shape-memory alloys, and carbon-nanotube-based
materials. Different element pairs define different constant-$\tau$ families,
so the target relaxation characteristic is selected by element choice alone,
while $n$ tunes the impedance magnitude along the chosen family.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the mechanism design
and working principle. Section~\ref{sec:modeling} derives the effective
impedance model. Section~\ref{sec:exp} reports the characterization
experiments and comparisons with viscoelastic baselines. Section~\ref{sec:concl}
concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Stiffness--Damping Co-Variation in Tissues and Materials}
Stiffness and damping are not independent in physical viscoelastic media;
they co-vary through a shared microstructural state. In vivo multifrequency
MR elastography shows that liver shear-wave speed rises steadily with
fibrosis, whereas the viscous loss angle remains largely unchanged from stage
F0 to F3 and increases only at cirrhosis (F4) \cite{reiter2021influence}.
In skeletal muscle, both the shear-wave elastic modulus and the viscosity
index (Viscosity PLUS) rise together after contraction, with moderate to
high positive correlation between the two measures
\cite{dulgheriu2022shear}. In the brain, the ratio of viscosity to
elasticity---reported as relative viscosity---is itself a physiologically
meaningful metric linked to memory performance
\cite{mcilvain2024contributions}, and the viscosity-sensitive dispersion
slope of shear waves distinguishes benign from malignant breast masses with
higher diagnostic accuracy than stiffness-based elastography alone
\cite{woo2026diagnostic}. Across cell and tissue scales, elasticity and
viscosity are governed by the same relaxation mechanisms
\cite{liu2024viscoelasticity}.

Materials research reaches the same conclusion. Since stiffness modulus and
loss tangent are usually mutually exclusive \cite{unwin2018escaping}, dense
stiff solids exhibit low damping and compliant polymers high damping; this
trade-off is captured by the stiffness--loss Ashby plot
\cite{tsimouri2024lightweight}. Attempts to escape this frontier include
negative-stiffness inclusions \cite{lakes2001extreme}, constrained high
internal-friction interfacial layers \cite{unwin2018escaping}, and submicron
viscoelastic interlayers in lightweight composites
\cite{tsimouri2024lightweight}; layered-composite optimization shows that
only a limited Pareto set achieves high stiffness and high damping
simultaneously \cite{meaud2013analysis}, and metamaterials such as
metadamping systems reshape, but do not remove, the coupling
\cite{hussein2013metadamping}. Carbon-nanotube assemblies exhibit
temperature-invariant viscoelasticity in which stiffness and damping both
derive from the same zipping--unzipping mechanism \cite{xu2010carbon}, and
self-healing constituents add further paths for tuning both quantities
together \cite{feng2025review}. In every case, within any given viscoelastic
system, stiffness and damping are locked by a common relaxation spectrum and
cannot be set independently.

\subsection{Co-Variation in Human Motor Control and Rehabilitation Robotics}
Human limbs display the same coupling. Antagonist co-contraction raises
joint stiffness, and the concurrent rise in muscle activation also raises
joint damping; subjects cannot fully suppress this coupling even when
instructed to maximize stiffness \cite{milner1995inability}. During reaching,
arm stiffness and viscosity both scale with muscle activation in a
task-dependent manner \cite{gomi1998task}, co-contraction modulates damping
directly and contributes to joint stability \cite{heitmann2011muscle}, and
reviews of mechanical impedance in motor control confirm that stiffness and
viscosity are co-produced by common activation patterns
\cite{mizrahi2015mechanical}.

Robotic counterparts reproduce this co-variation, but only through coupled
identification and dual-loop control. Impedance-learning adaptive control
identifies human-limb impedance during interaction \cite{sharifi2022impedance};
fuzzy-forgetting recursive least squares predicts upper-limb impedance
parameters on-line \cite{tao2023research}; hybrid adaptive controllers
periodically estimate both human stiffness and damping
\cite{jiang2024impedance}; surface-EMG-based arm-end stiffness estimation
drives variable impedance control \cite{wang2025adaptive}; and soft exosuits
are characterized by both their stiffness and damping properties
\cite{bottin2024characterization}. On the identification side, stiffness and
damping are therefore coupled, as in biological tissue; on the control side,
however, they remain separately synthesized quantities, each with its own
estimation loop and stability margin. The co-variation that is natural to
the human limb is never realized by a single physical parameter.

\subsection{Constitutive Theory: Why One Parameter Governs Both}
Constitutive theory explains why a single parameter should govern both
quantities. Soft tissues exhibit a relaxation time constant that is largely
invariant over a physiological range of strain rates
\cite{fun1980biomechanics}, and power-law rheology describes cell and tissue
mechanics with a single fractional exponent controlling the frequency
dependence of both the storage and the loss modulus
\cite{bonfanti2020fractional}. For haptic rendering, a single fractional
spring-damper element simultaneously displays stiffness and damping, and its
parameters exhibit an unintuitive, frequency-dependent coupling
\cite{tolasa2025haptic}; passivity conditions for such fractional-order
rendering have been derived together with closed-form expressions for the
effective stiffness and damping rendered to the user
\cite{gemalmaz2026haptic}. These results generalize the classical
Kelvin--Voigt, Maxwell, and standard linear solid models and confirm that
the stiffness and damping experienced by a user are two facets of a single
constitutive law.

\subsection{Variable Impedance Mechanisms and Haptic Rendering Baselines}
Variable impedance actuators adjust stiffness and damping through separate
modules or separate control inputs \cite{vanderborght2013variable}.
Variable-transmission designs, such as the mechanically adjustable series
elastic actuator, enlarge the achievable impedance range but remain
stiffness-only \cite{mehling2007increasing}. In haptic rendering, the
stability of a rendered virtual wall is bounded by the Z-width of the
display \cite{colgate1994factors}; discrete-time passivity imposes
fundamental limits on the achievable virtual stiffness and damping
\cite{colgate1997passivity}; and the stability boundary depends on both the
wall damping and the loop delay \cite{hulin2006stability,hulin2009stability}.
Moreover, damping rendered in free space and at the wall systematically
biases human classification of virtual wall stiffness
\cite{treadway2024effects}, confirming that damping is perceptually as
relevant as stiffness. Quasi-passive stiffness displays based on
variable-length-lever transmissions achieve a wide controllable stiffness
range while decoupling the output stiffness from the press displacement
\cite{shi2025quasipassive}; as stiffness-only transmissions, however, they
provide no velocity-dependent damping channel.

In all of the above work, the rendered impedance occupies an arbitrary point
of the stiffness--damping plane: the two quantities are independently
programmed, and the relaxation time constant is never treated as a design
parameter. This paper instead treats $\tau$ as the invariant of a
single-parameter family: the lever ratio $n$ co-regulates
$k_{\mathrm{eq}}=k_s/n^{2}$ and $c_{\mathrm{eq}}=c_{tr}/n^{2}$ along a
constant-$\tau$ line, with $\tau=c_{tr}/k_s$ set by the choice of the
passive element pair. The mechanism thus reproduces, from one
geometric parameter,
the co-variation law that tissues, materials, the human motor system, and
constitutive theory all exhibit---without active damping synthesis.

\subsection{Mechanism Layout}
The mechanism consists of a lever with a continuously adjustable pivot and a
pair of passive elements---a linear spring $k_s$ and a translational dashpot
$c_{tr}$---nested in parallel at the load side of the transmission, a
distance $l_1$ from the pivot; the user port is located at a distance
$l_0-l_1$ from the pivot. The lever ratio
\begin{equation}
n = \frac{l_0 - l_1}{l_1} \label{eq:leverratio}
\end{equation}
is therefore modulated continuously by translating the pivot along the
lever. Following the variable-length-lever design of quasi-passive stiffness
displays \cite{shi2025quasipassive}, the effective moment arms are
configured so that the spring and dashpot forces always act perpendicular to
the lever over a constant working length: the element torques are
independent of the operating angle and depend only on the port velocity and
the pivot position. The stiffness and damping seen at the port are thus
decoupled from the angle of the lever by construction, and both quantities
share the same geometric pivot as the single modulation source.

\subsection{Working Principle}
When the user presses the port through a displacement $h_p$ at velocity
$\dot{h}_p$, the transmission maps the port motion onto the element strokes
by the lever ratio \eqref{eq:leverratio}. The nested spring--dashpot pair
reacts with the sum of its elastic and dissipative forces, which is
reflected back through the lever to produce the effective impedance
$k_{\mathrm{eq}}h_p + c_{\mathrm{eq}}\dot{h}_p$ at the user port, as derived
in Section~\ref{sec:modeling}. Translating the pivot changes $n$ and thereby
scales the effective stiffness and damping jointly, while their ratio---the
relaxation time constant---is left intact because both elements scale with
the same power of the lever ratio.

\section{Modeling of Effective Impedance}\label{sec:modeling}
As described in Section~\ref{sec:design}, the constant working length of the
transmission couples the element strokes to the port motion through the
lever ratio alone: the rendered impedance is decoupled from the operating
angle by construction. Let $h_s$ denote the stroke of the nested
spring--dashpot pair, $F_L = k_s\,h_s + c_{tr}\,\dot{h}_s$ the force
generated by the pair, and $F_B$ the force applied by the user at the port.
By the levering of \eqref{eq:leverratio},
\begin{equation}
h_s = \frac{h_p}{n}, \qquad F_B = \frac{F_L}{n}. \label{eq:lever}
\end{equation}
Substituting the first relation into $F_L$ and reflecting the load-side
force through the second relation yields the force--displacement relation at
the user port
\begin{equation}
F_B = \frac{k_s}{n^{2}}\,h_p + \frac{c_{tr}}{n^{2}}\,\dot{h}_p ,
\label{eq:userforce}
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to a parallel spring--damper of effective parameters
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{k_s}{n^{2}}, \qquad
c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{c_{tr}}{n^{2}}. \label{eq:effective}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Single-Parameter Co-Regulation}
The key property of \eqref{eq:effective} is that a single parameter, the
lever ratio $n$, co-regulates both quantities with the same functional
dependence. Consequently, the stiffness-to-damping ratio
\begin{equation}
\frac{c_{\mathrm{eq}}}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \frac{c_{tr}}{k_s}
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
and the relaxation time constant
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac{c_{\mathrm{eq}}}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \frac{c_{tr}}{k_s}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
are invariant under modulation and depend only on the passive element pair.
The rendered impedance therefore belongs to the family
\begin{equation}
Z_{\mathrm{eq}}(s) = \frac{k_s}{n^{2}}\left(1 + \tau\, s\right),
\label{eq:impedance}
\end{equation}
whose magnitude is tuned by $n$ while its characteristic time scale is
frozen by construction. This reproduces the co-variation of stiffness and
damping observed in physical viscoelastic media, where the relaxation time
is largely material-specific rather than modulation-dependent.

% 被动性说明
\subsection{Intrinsic Passivity}
Because both elements are passive and the transmission is purely
mechanical, the rendered impedance is intrinsically passive: the energy
delivered to the user port is bounded by the energy initially stored in the
spring and dissipated by the dashpot. No velocity estimation, no active
force injection, and no stability margin are required. The energy balance
reads
\begin{equation}
\int_0^t F_B\,\dot{h}_p\,dt = \frac{1}{2}\,k_{\mathrm{eq}}\,h_p^2(t)
+ \int_0^t c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\dot{h}_p^2\,dt \ge 0 ,
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
for any admissible trajectory, which is the passivity condition in the sense
of \cite{colgate1994factors}.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Coverage and the Constant-Relaxation-Time Family}
A single lever ratio renders one point on a fixed constant-$\tau$ line, so it
is fair to ask whether one $\tau$ suffices. First, the constraint that the
rendered impedance be bounded is not unique to our mechanism: active
impedance control is itself constrained by the discrete-time passivity
condition and by the Z-width of the display
\cite{colgate1997passivity,colgate1994factors}. Our restriction is a hard
structural one---the device is unconditionally passive by construction---whereas
active systems must trade stiffness or damping to preserve a soft,
loop-dependent stability margin. Second, $\tau$ is a design parameter:
choosing a different (spring, damper) element pair yields a different
constant-$\tau$ family, and since both effective quantities scale with the
same power of the lever ratio $n$, the stiffness--damping plane can be
covered by a finite set of
families aimed at the relaxation characteristics of specific materials
(e.g., biological tissue versus NiTi). Third, the controllable engagement of
the dissipative element provides a slow-scale second channel: changing the
damper adjusts $c$ and hence $\tau$, at the price of a one-time
recalibration rather than an online coupling loop. Fourth, we report the
constant-$\tau$ limitation explicitly: rendering points off the current
family requires a physical change of the element pair, which is the
mechanical analogue of the spectrum change that real materials undergo when
their microstructure changes.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Limitations include the achievable bandwidth of the damper engagement
mechanism, the coupling boundary between $n$ and the range of the damping
element, and the precision of the prototype. Future work will extend the
coverage through multi-pivot and multi-ratio transmissions, integrate
sensing, and address non-affine loads and the temperature sensitivity of the
dissipative element.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
This paper proposed a passive viscoelastic rendering mechanism in which a
spring and a controllable damper are integrated at the load side of a
variable lever-ratio transmission. A single lever ratio simultaneously
co-regulates the effective stiffness and damping while keeping the
relaxation time constant invariant under modulation, reproducing the
co-varying viscoelastic behavior of physical materials without active
velocity estimation or software-synthesized damping. The relaxation time
constant is set by the passive element pair, so
different constant-$\tau$ families can be aimed at different material
classes. Future work will complete prototype characterization and
quantitative comparison with biological tissue.
